\section{Prototypes and Categories}

\begin{frame}{Prototypes}
  \Large
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Categories are organized around a \txx{prototype}
  \item What is a typical \con{bird}? A typical piece of \con{furniture}?
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    a typical \con{bird} & has feathers, warbles, flies, lays eggs \\
    a penguin           & has feathers, lays eggs \ldots \\
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Pioneered by \citet{rosch:1973} and
  \citet{rosch:mervis:gray:johnson:boyes-braem:1976} (very readable)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Relations between Concepts}
  \Large
  \begin{itemize}
  \item The commonest link is \txx{hypernymy}: a \con{dog} is an \con{animal}
  \item Subordinate concepts \txx{inherit} from superordinate ones
  \item Larger units of knowledge are \txx{frames}
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Built as resources: WordNet \citep{_fellbaum:1998},
  FrameNet \citep{framenet:2002}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Basic Level}
  \Large
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    superordinate & \alert{basic} & subordinate \\
    \midrule
    \lex{vehicle} & \lex{bus}      & \lex{school bus} \\
    \lex{jewelry} & \lex{necklace} & \lex{bib necklace} \\
    \lex{animal}  & \lex{dog}      & \lex{poodle} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Basic-level names are recognized fastest, used most, learned first,
  short, and common in compounds
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Most Informative Level}
  \Large
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Members share \txx{many} attributes with each other
  \item \ldots and \txx{few} with other categories
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Where the level sits varies: \lex{dog} is basic at the species level, but
  \lex{bird} and \lex{fish} are higher --- and it differs between groups of
  people.  Frequency is closely tied to the basic level, but not the same.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Linguistic Relativity}
  \large
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Does what is \txx{easy to say} affect how we \txx{think}?
  \item Colours are easier to tell apart if you have names for them
  \item English and Chinese speakers differ on substances vs individuals
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \multicolumn{2}{l}{\lex{key} is described as \ldots} \\
    \midrule
    German (masculine) & hard, heavy, jagged, metal, useful \\
    Spanish (feminine) & golden, intricate, little, lovely, shiny, tiny \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Most confirmed differences are very, very subtle
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Sapir--Whorf Hypothesis}
  \Large
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \txx{strong} & language \emph{determines} thought \\
    \txx{weak}   & language \emph{influences} thought and behaviour \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Sapir and Whorf never wrote together, and never stated a hypothesis ---
  let alone two versions of one
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Whorf Actually Said}
\begin{quotation}
  We dissect nature along lines laid down by our native language. The
  categories and types that we isolate from the world of phenomena we
  do not find there because they stare every observer in the face; on
  the contrary, the world is presented in a kaleidoscope flux of
  impressions which has to be organized by our minds---and this means
  largely by the linguistic systems of our minds. We cut nature up,
  organize it into concepts, and ascribe significances as we do,
  largely because we are parties to an agreement to organize it in
  this way---an agreement that holds throughout our speech community and
  is codified in the patterns of our language [\ldots] all observers
  are not led by the same physical evidence to the same picture of the
  universe, unless their linguistic backgrounds are similar, or can in
  some way be calibrated.
\\ \hfill Whorf (Carroll; Ed.); 1956: pp.\ 212--214
\end{quotation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Metaphor Is Everywhere}
  \Large
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \eng{The price of sprouts went \alert{up}.} & \eng{I \alert{killed} his argument.} \\

  \end{tabular}
  \vfill

\begin{frame}{\textit{Metaphors We Live By}}
  \begin{quote}
    \Large Our conceptual system is fundamentally metaphorical in nature
  \end{quote}
  \hfill \citet{lakoff:johnson:1980}
  \vfill
  \Large
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \txx{conventional} & well established, yet still metaphorical \\
    \txx{systematic}   & part of larger domains \\
    \txx{asymmetrical} & the \emp{abstract} in terms of the \emp{concrete} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Cognitive semantics: no separation between cognition and linguistic knowledge
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Good Metaphors: Not Too Close, Not Too Far}
  \normalsize
  \begin{tabular}{ll>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.2cm}}
    \toprule
    too similar & too different & just right \\
    \midrule
    \eng{$^\#$Wine is whisky} & \eng{$^\#$His feet were stars} & \eng{Life is a subway train} \\
    \eng{$^\#$Cars are trucks} & \eng{$^\#$Her cheeks were typewriters} & \eng{Men are thistles} \\
    \eng{$^\#$Jam is honey}  & \eng{$^\#$Her knees were penguins} & \eng{He posted the toast down to his stomach} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Different fields, sharing one minor characteristic: the dissimilarity tells

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Source and Target Domains}
  \begin{quote}
    Metaphors enable us to understand one domain of experience in terms of
    another. \hfill \citet{lakoff:turner:1989}
  \end{quote}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}
      \node[draw, rounded corners, minimum width=3cm, minimum height=1cm,
            font=\Large] (s) at (0,0) {JOURNEY};
      \node[draw, rounded corners, minimum width=3cm, minimum height=1cm,
            font=\Large] (t) at (6,0) {LIFE};
      \draw[->, very thick, teal] (s) -- node[above] {mapping} (t);
      \node[below=2pt of s] {\txx{source}: concrete};

    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \large
  Written \txx{TARGET} is \txx{SOURCE}: LIFE is a JOURNEY.  The mapping is
  \emp{systematic}: a whole structure is carried over, not a single word
\end{frame}

\section{Spatial and Orientational Metaphors}

\begin{frame}{Spatial Metaphors}
  \Large
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Give a concept an orientation: \lex{up--down}, \lex{in--out},
    \lex{front--back}, \lex{deep--shallow}
  \item HAPPY is UP: \eng{I'm feeling \alert{up} today}
  \item Physical, but they can still \txx{vary}
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  In most cultures the FUTURE is in FRONT; for the Aymara of the Andes
  (Bolivia, Peru, Chile) it is \emph{behind}
\end{frame}

\section{Metonymy}

\begin{frame}{Metonymy: A Stands for B}
  \Large
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \eng{\alert{The White House} announced \ldots} & building $\to$ institution \\
    \eng{She enjoys reading \alert{Shakespeare}.} & author $\to$ works \\
    \eng{The \alert{kettle} is boiling.}          & container $\to$ contents \\
    \eng{All \alert{hands} on deck.}              & part $\to$ whole person \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  A shift of reference \emph{within} one domain, based on \txx{contiguity},
  not similarity: metonymy navigates structure that is already there
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Metonymy vs.\ Metaphor}
  \Large
  \begin{tabular}{lll}
    \toprule
    & metonymy & metaphor \\
    \midrule
    domains & within one     & across two \\
    basis   & contiguity     & similarity \\
    does    & refers         & structures \\
    example & \eng{\alert{Brussels} decided \ldots} & \eng{He \alert{attacked} my argument.} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Metonymy: A \emph{stands for} B.  Metaphor: A is \emph{understood in terms
  of} B
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Kinds of Metonymy}
  \normalsize
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    PART for WHOLE (synecdoche)  & \eng{Can you lend a \alert{hand}?} \\

    MATERIAL for OBJECT          & \eng{I won \alert{gold}.} \\
    PRODUCER for PRODUCT         & \eng{I'll get an \alert{IBM}.} \\
    PLACE for INSTITUTION        & \eng{\alert{Downing Street} has made no comment.} \\

    PLACE for EVENT              & \eng{\alert{Hiroshima} changed our view of war.} \\

    OBJECT for USER              & \eng{They are a hired \alert{gun}.} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{frame}

\section{Case Study: Anger}

\begin{frame}{Anger Has a Physiology}
  \large
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    body heat          & \eng{hot under the collar}, \eng{a hothead}, \eng{a heated argument} \\
    internal pressure  & \eng{I almost burst a blood vessel}, \eng{I exploded at them} \\
    redness            & \eng{scarlet with rage}, \eng{flushed with anger} \\
    agitation          & \eng{shaking with anger}, \eng{hopping mad}, \eng{all worked up} \\
    blurred perception & \eng{blind with rage}, \eng{seeing red}, \eng{couldn't see straight} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Emotions are not just feelings: they have conceptual structure, visible
  in metaphor.  Lakoff and Johnson's cultural model of anger: the effects
  grow with the anger, up to a limit beyond which we cannot function
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Body Is a Container}
  \Large
  \begin{tabular}{l}
    \eng{He was \alert{filled} with anger.} \\
    \eng{She couldn't \alert{contain} her joy.} \\
    \eng{She was \alert{brimming} with rage.} \\
    \eng{Get the anger \alert{out of your system}.} \\
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Combine it with ANGER is HEAT and you get the central metaphor:
  ANGER is a \txx{HOT FLUID in a CONTAINER}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{ANGER is a Hot Fluid in a Container}
  \large
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    cool = calm    & \eng{Keep \alert{cool}.} \eng{Stay calm.} \\
    heat           & \eng{You make my blood \alert{boil}.} \eng{\alert{Simmer} down!} \eng{She was \alert{seething}.} \\
    rising         & \eng{His anger \alert{welled up}.} \eng{Pretty soon I was in a \alert{towering} rage.} \\
    pressure       & \eng{He was \alert{bursting} with anger.} \eng{He kept it \alert{bottled up}.} \\
    explosion      & \eng{She \alert{blew up} at me.} \eng{She \alert{blew a fuse}.} \eng{He \alert{erupted}.} \\
    parts fly off  & \eng{I \alert{blew my top}.} \eng{She \alert{flipped her lid}.} \eng{He \alert{hit the ceiling}.} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  \lex{pissed off} is only used for anger: hot liquid under pressure
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Container Has a Limit}
  \Large
  \begin{itemize}
  \item A closed container takes only so much heat: the anger scale has a
    \txx{limit}
  \item The metaphor is motivated by our \txx{physiology}
  \item Other metaphors exist too:
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \large
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    ANGER is FIRE     & ANGER is an OPPONENT \\
    ANGER is INSANITY & ANGER is a DANGEROUS ANIMAL \\
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Japanese and Korean: Belly, Head and Fire}

  \begin{tabular}{lll}

    \midrule
    \kos{화가 나다}  & \kor{hwa-ga nada}    & get angry (``fire comes out'') \\
    \kos{속이 타다}  & \kor{sok-i tada}     & fret (``the insides burn'') \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Korean \kor{hwa} is Sino-Korean \zht{火} ``fire''.  The same broad
  picture --- container, heat, something rising --- with different body
  parts and substances.  Where does anger live in your language?
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Universal and Variable}
  \Large
  \begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    \emp{universal} & shared bodies: the pressurized container \\
    \emp{variable}  & the substance: fluid, \emph{qi}, chilli \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  \citet{kovecses2005metaphor}.  Two easy mistakes: calling a metaphor
  universal because English and one other language have it, or culturally
  specific because you haven't found it elsewhere yet
\end{frame}

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{frame}{Conclusions}
  \Large
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Categories have \txx{prototypes}; metaphor and metonymy extend them
  \item Metaphor is not decoration: it structures \txx{argument, time,
      emotion} and \txx{people}
  \item East and Southeast Asia share the broad mappings, realized with
    different \txx{bodies, substances} and \txx{animals}
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \normalsize
  Idioms we learn; metaphors we work out on the fly --- the line is fuzzy.
  \citet{saeed:2015}: Metaphor 11.4, Metonymy 11.5
\end{frame}
