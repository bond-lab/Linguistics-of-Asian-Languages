  \begin{itemize}
  \item From the Indian subcontinent to the Indonesian archipelago:
    \begin{itemize}
    \item about \txx{2{,}000 languages} --- roughly \txx{30\%} of the
      7,000 spoken on the planet
    \item about \txx{one third of humanity}
    \end{itemize}
  \item South Asia alone: \txx{1.8 billion} people in eight countries,
    speaking some \txx{664} languages
  \item Eight or so phyla: Austroasiatic, Austronesian, Dravidian,
    Hmong-Mien, Indo-Aryan, Sino-Tibetan, Tai-Kadai, and several Papuan
    families
  \item Plus isolates with no known relatives: \txx{Burushaski} (N.\ Pakistan),
    \txx{Nihali} (central-west India), \txx{Kusunda} (western Nepal)
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020; Hammarström et al.\ 2018; Eberhard et al.\ 2019}

\section{South Asia}

\begin{frame}{Who was in South Asia first?}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Five families, plus three isolates:
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \txx{Austroasiatic} (Munda, Khasic), \txx{Dravidian},
      \txx{Indo-European} (Indo-Aryan, Iranian, Nuristani),
      \txx{Sino-Tibetan} (Tibeto-Burman), \txx{Tai-Kadai}
    \end{itemize}
  \item \txx{Munda} speakers predate the Indo-Aryan migration.  Place names in
    the Dravidian Kurukh- and Malto-speaking regions are of Munda origin
  \item \txx{Dravidian} likely is native to the region it is spoken in
    (autochthonous), and Proto-Dravidian may have been the language of
    the Indus Civilization.  Non-Indo-Aryan place names (toponyms) survive in
    northern India
  \item The Khasi and Jaintia Hills languages look like a \emph{later} arrival
    from the east --- their features are MSEA-like, unlike Munda
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020: 19--21}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dating the Indo-Aryan arrival from a text}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item The \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigveda}{\txx{Ṛgveda}} (1500-1000 BCE) makes \emph{no reference to iron} --- so it predates
    c.\ 1000 BCE, when iron appears in South Asia
  \item It refers to the cities of the Indus Valley Civilization only as
    \emph{ruins} --- so it postdates their collapse around 1900 BCE
  \item That brackets both the text and the Aryan migration at
    roughly \txx{1500 BCE}
  \item Dravidian loanwords suggest early contact in Vedic Sanskrit:
    \emph{ulūkhala-} `mortar', \emph{kuṇḍa} `pit', \emph{khála-} `threshing
    floor', \emph{kāṇa} `one-eyed', \emph{mayūra} `peacock'
  \item[!]  \emph{What a text does not mention} can date it
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Witzel 2001; Emeneau 1954; Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020: 20, 29}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Assam: a Tai kingdom that left an Indo-Aryan language}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item In the 13th century a tribe of Shans crossed the Patkai range and
    founded a kingdom that lasted 600 years.  Their language, \txx{Ahom},
    gives \txx{Assam} its name
  \item Ahom is now dead, but modern \txx{Assamese} --- an Indo-Aryan language
    --- carries its imprint:
    \begin{itemize}
    \item it has \emph{lost} the dental\,$\sim$\,retroflex contrast in stops,
      unusual for Indo-Aryan, though the orthography still writes both
    \item it has an extensive \emph{numeral classifier} system, common in MSEA
      but only rudimentary elsewhere in Indo-Aryan
    \end{itemize}
  \item River names preserve an older layer still: Tai \emph{nam} `water' in
    the Namphuk, and a \emph{di/ti} formative in the Dehing, Dikhu, Disang,
    Tizü and Tiho --- from Proto-Tibeto-Burman \emph{*ti(y)} `water'
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Gait 1906; Benedict 1972; Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020: 21--22}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Zomia: the uplands as refuge}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Scott (2009) calls the extensive uplands of Southeast Asia and beyond
    \txx{Zomia}: historically a refuge zone for minorities fleeing corvée (intermittent forced unpaid)
    labour and taxation imposed by the lowland padi states
  \item In MSEA the main booty of a victorious campaign was \emph{manpower}.
    The Thai expression is \emph{\ipa{kwàːt mɯːang}}, literally `sweep city'
  \item Whole communities were carried off to Tai and Burmese padi states to
    work the wet rice fields --- and concentrated labour encourages a shift to
    the language of the rulers
  \item Lowlands consolidate into few big languages, uplands preserve
    many small ones
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Scott 2009; Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020: 22, 42}
\end{frame}

\section{Contact, Trade and Writing}

\begin{frame}{Indosphere and Sinosphere}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item India and China are large adjacent states that have always exerted
    considerable sway over the interface area of Southeast Asia
  \item Matisoff (1991) coined \txx{Indosphere} and \txx{Sinosphere} for their
    overlapping domains of cultural and linguistic influence
  \item Blust's four periods of influence: \txx{(i) Indian}, \txx{(ii)
    Chinese}, \txx{(iii) Islamic}, \txx{(iv) European}
  \item Chinese contact with Southeast Asia was \emph{commercial}: it did not
    spread Chinese literacy southward, as it did to Korea, Japan and northern
    Vietnam
  \item[?] Which sphere is a given language in?  Often both, in different
    parts of the lexicon
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Matisoff 1991; Blust 2009; Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020: 27, 31}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three degrees of contact}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item From weakest to strongest:
  \item \txx{Lexical borrowing} --- a word is transferred from one language to
    another.  Religious terms from Hinduism, Buddhism and Islam diffused across
    the whole area this way
  \item \txx{Structural borrowing / convergence} --- the organising principles
    of two languages align, in phonology, in word order, in pragmatics.
    Bilingual, cohabiting speakers.  (The particle \emph{lah} in Singapore
    Colloquial English, Malay and Chinese.)
  \item \txx{Language shift} --- the subjugated community gives up its language
    for the dominant one.  The substrate survives in place names and specialist
    vocabulary
  \item Shift is now happening across the whole region in favour of national
    languages, and is expected to reduce diversity sharply this century
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020: 28--29}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three contact zones}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \txx{The Indian subcontinent} --- Indo-Aryan meets Dravidian.  The
    South Asian areal features (dental\,$\sim$\,retroflex contrast, suffixing
    morphology, verb-final order, dative subjects) may largely be a contact
    outcome
  \item \txx{Mainland Southeast Asia} --- five families, and
    \emph{the greatest extent of linguistic convergence on the planet}
    (Enfield 2011).  A single grammatical template can replicate the same
    sentence in unrelated languages; often only the words differ
  \item \txx{Around New Guinea} --- Austronesian meets Papuan, giving the
    East Nusantara area.  Both sides underwent structural simplification
  \item Expanding agriculturists usually absorbed hunter-gatherers --- but not
    where they met other food producers: the \txx{Neolithic standoff} of
    eastern Indonesia
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Bisang 1996; Klamer 2004, 2012; Lansing et al.\ 2011; Enfield 2011}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Malay: the lingua franca for two millennia}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Malay was the de facto \txx{lingua franca of island Southeast Asia for
    almost two thousand years}, documented in stone inscriptions from the
    7th century CE
  \item It was \emph{not} displaced by Javanese, Chinese, Arabic, Portuguese or
    Dutch --- \txx{only by English, around the time of the Second World War}
  \item It carried the prestige of the Hindu, Buddhist and Islamic periods, and
    was the main conduit for Islam
  \item \emph{masuk Melayu} `enter Malay' meant both converting to Islam and
    shifting to the Malay language
  \item Malay was also the \emph{intermediary} for Sanskrit, Tamil, Persian and
    Arabic loans across the region --- which is why so many charts later in this
    lecture run through Malay
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Adelaar \& Prentice 1996; Blust 2009; Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020: 33--34}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Vietnamese: the one script imposed by conquest}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Writing reached MSEA and ISEA with religious texts, in
    \txx{Brāhmī-based abugidas} from South India.  Burmese, Khmer, Lao, Mon,
    Shan and Thai --- essentially every Theravada Buddhist language --- use
    them
  \item \txx{Vietnamese is the exception}: the only Austroasiatic language of
    MSEA that did not use an Indic-derived script, and the only language of the
    region to have a writing system \emph{imposed by a conquering power}
  \item Classical Chinese logographs after the Han invasion of 111 BCE, then
    \txx{chữ Nôm}, with characters added for what Chinese could not write
  \item 17th-century Catholic missionaries devised a Roman script with
    diacritics for the tones that had developed under Sinitic domination; the
    French administration made it official in the early 20th century
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Court 1996; Nguyễn 1996; Coupe \& Kratochvíl 2020: 31, 34}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Wanderwörter}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item A \txx{Wanderwort} is a word that has travelled far beyond the family
    it started in, passed from language to language along a trade route rather
    than inherited
  \item They are typically \emph{acculturation terms}: trade goods, ritual
    objects, new technologies.  Few in number, but geographically enormous
  \item They are a nuisance for the comparative method --- they look like
    cognates but are not, and they can make unrelated languages look related
  \item Ones you already know: \emph{tea} (Min \emph{tê} by sea,
    Mandarin \emph{chá} by land), \emph{sugar}, \emph{ginger}, \emph{orange}
  \item In SEA,  Malay was the \emph{vehicle} for such
    words --- Sanskrit, Tamil, Persian and Arabic loans all rode it
  \end{itemize}
  \asrc{Haynie et al.\ 2014}
\end{frame}

\section{Conclusion}

\begin{frame}{Asia before colonial times}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item The region is bounded by the Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean, the Pacific,
    and the Himalaya --- and governed throughout by the \txx{monsoon}, which
    set what could be grown, when ships could sail, and so where people and
    languages went
  \item The modern language map is largely the map of \txx{early agriculturists
    expanding}, channelled by obstacles (mountains, rainforest, sea) and
    gateways (river valleys, island chains, straits)
  \item Where they could not reach, diversity survived: \txx{Zomia}, and the
    Papuan\,--\,Austronesian standoff in the east
  \item Contact has been \emph{continuous and multidirectional} for millennia
    --- Indic, Sinitic, Arabic, Malay --- leaving loanwords, converged grammars,
    scripts, and whole languages abandoned
  \item By the start of our era the region was already linked to the Arabic and
    Sinitic worlds, with Malay its lingua franca
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Context is important}
  \begin{quote}
    \ldots\ the European colonial powers \ldots\ contributed merely the latest
    layer of color on the linguistically and culturally rich canvas of the
    region.
  \end{quote}
  \begin{flushright}
    Coupe \& Kratochvíl (2020: 42)
  \end{flushright}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item English is not the first lingua franca here, nor the first prestige
    language, nor the first source of loanwords.  It is the most recent
  \item Malay held that role for almost two thousand years, and was displaced
    only around the Second World War
  \item The patterns we will see in Asian Englishes --- borrowing, convergence,
    shift --- are the same three processes, running again
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
